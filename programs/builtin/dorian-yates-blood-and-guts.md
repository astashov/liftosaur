Blood and Guts is the high-intensity training (HIT) routine six-time Mr. Olympia Dorian Yates used to win the 1995, 1996 and 1997 Olympias. It's a 4-day split, where every muscle group is trained once a week. For the first exercise of every muscle group, you do 1-3 warm-up sets. Then there's only a single max effort working set per exercise - you go to failure, and often past it too, with forced reps, partials or rest-pause. Weights are moderate (8-10 reps), reps are slow and controlled, and when you get to the top of the rep range - you increase the weight.

### Origin & Philosophy

Dorian Yates won six Mr. Olympia titles in a row, from 1992 to 1997. Yates trained in Temple Gym in Birmingham, England, and his training style got famous thanks to his 1993 book *Blood and Guts*, and a 1996 workout video with the same name. So that's where the program name comes from. The exact routine is taken from [*A Portrait of Dorian Yates*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/218809) (1998), which Yates co-wrote with FLEX editor Peter McGough (in the US it was released as *A Warrior's Story*). It lists the whole split - all exercises, warm-up sets, rep ranges, and even actual weights Yates used. According to Yates, that's the schedule he followed "from 1995 onward", and he calls it "my most productive" program.

Yates built his approach on Mike Mentzer's [Heavy Duty](/programs/mike-mentzer-heavy-duty). The main idea: one set at 100% effort sends the growth signal, and every extra set just eats into recovery. He compares it to hammering a nail - if you drove it in with the first hit, hitting it again only damages the wood. He also argues that with 3 sets you always pace yourself and hold back on the first two.

It wasn't like that from the beginning though. As a beginner, Yates did 3 sets per exercise, then 2 sets per exercise from 1986, and in 1992 switched to a single main set. Frequency also went down while getting stronger - each body part was trained on a 5-day cycle (1987-1991), then a 6-day cycle (1992), and a 7-day cycle from 1994. After tearing a biceps and injuring a shoulder in 1994, Yates made 3 more changes, and tells readers to use them "from day one":

- Smaller weights and a bit higher reps - you'd hit full exhaustion around 8-10 reps, instead of 6-8
- Slow, controlled reps - lower the weight slowly, then raise it evenly, don't explode from the bottom
- More machine-based exercises, since you can push them to the limit with less chance of getting injured

### Who It's For

- **Experience level**: Advanced. Yates says it took him 9 years (1983-1992) to learn how to give 100% in a single set, and for novices he recommends 3 sets per exercise. Also, he didn't use forced reps until the second year of lifting. If you have less than 2-3 years of serious lifting experience, you should start with some other program.
- **Prerequisites**: You should know what true failure feels like, and have solid technique on all the exercises. Ideally, you'd also have a partner for forced reps.
- **Goal**: Hypertrophy. Strength would increase as well, but the program doesn't have any low-rep sets or 1RM testing. Yates said he never attempted a 1-rep max, ever.
- **Cut/bulk**: Both. The volume is low, so it's a good choice for a cut, when your ability to recover is limited.

### Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Very little volume - 1 working set per exercise, 8 working sets on back and leg days, 13 on the other two (6 of them abs). Sessions take 30-50 minutes.
- Every muscle gets a whole week to recover, which suits people who can't recover from high-volume programs
- Uses machines heavily, which makes going to failure safer than with free weights
- Simple progression: hit the top of the rep range, add weight
- Detailed and well documented - Yates printed every exercise, warm-up and weight he used

**Cons**

- Each muscle gets hit only 1x/week. Modern research suggests 2x/week is better for most people.
- Low number of sets per week: 1-4 direct hard sets per muscle group per week (usually 3-4). If you respond well to more sets, you may grow slower on it.
- Forced reps and negatives need a partner. If you work out alone, you're limited to rest-pause, partials and drop sets.
- With a single hard set there's zero room for a bad set. If you leave anything in the tank, you get almost nothing from that workout.
- Needs a well-equipped gym: Hammer Strength equipment, Smith machine, cables, leg press, hack squat, calf machines
- Going to failure on big compounds (deadlift, barbell rows) is mentally tough and pretty risky injury-wise. Yates himself tore his biceps doing barbell rows with 405lb

### Program Structure

- **Split**: 4-way body part split - Delts/Traps/Triceps, Back/Rear Delts, Chest/Biceps, Legs. Plus abs twice a week.
- **Periodization**: None. The exercises stay the same week to week, and it uses double progression (i.e. you add reps, and once you hit the top of the range - bump the weight).
- **Schedule**: Fixed weekly schedule - 4 workouts and 3 rest days:
  - Day 1: Delts, traps, triceps, abs
  - Day 2: Back, rear delts, lower back
  - Day 3: Rest
  - Day 4: Chest, biceps, abs
  - Day 5: Rest
  - Day 6: Quads, hamstrings, calves
  - Day 7: Rest
- **2-week cycle**: The only difference between the weeks is the first exercise on Day 2. Yates used to alternate machine pulldowns and Nautilus pullovers between pulling workouts, so on week 1 you do [{Lat Pulldown, Leverage Machine}], and on week 2 - [{Pullover}].

Biceps go 2 days after the back day, not the next day. Yates says biceps get hit pretty hard on the back day anyway, from all the pulling, so he recommends not doing biceps the day after back.

### Exercise Selection & Rationale

**Delts, traps, triceps**: [{Shoulder Press, Smith Machine}] is the main press here, the one where you go with big weights. Smith machine locks the bar path, so you can safely go to failure on it. Then there're 2 lateral variations for the middle delts - seated [{Lateral Raise}] with DBs, and one-arm [{Lateral Raise, Cable}] (because that way you get tension at the bottom part, and with DBs you don't). [{Shrug}] with DBs for traps. And 3 exercises for tris: [{Triceps Pushdown}], [{Skullcrusher}] with EZ-bar, and one-arm reverse-grip pushdown (Yates did one-arm Nautilus extension there instead).

**Back**: First goes the pulldown/pullover. Yates did [{Pullover}] on a Nautilus machine, since it isolates the lats and doesn't let biceps take over. Closest substitute is a dumbbell pullover. Then [{Bent Over Row}], with overhand grip (after his biceps tear), and a 1-second squeeze at the top. After that, 2 more: a single arm Hammer Strength version ([{Seated Row, Leverage Machine}]) and a cable version with overhand grip ([{Seated Row}]). Rear delts go here too, since they're warmed up anyway after all that pulling - [{Reverse Fly, Leverage Machine}] and bent-over dumbbell [{Reverse Fly}]. Then the day ends with the lower back - [{Back Extension}] and [{Deadlift}]. Extensions are deliberately done right in front of deadlifts - that pre-exhausts the lower back, so Yates could go to failure on deadlifts with less weight and lower chance of getting injured. Deadlifts are done for 8 reps, and that's it - without any forced reps/partials.

**Chest, biceps**: [{Incline Bench Press}] was Yates' main lift for growing pec size, and it gets 3 warmup sets, the most of any exercise in the program. Then there's [{Chest Press, Leverage Machine}] (a Hammer Strength flat press) for the second press. [{Incline Chest Fly}] and [{Cable Crossover}] finish off the pecs, without involving triceps. For biceps - [{Incline Curl}] for the long head stretch, [{Bicep Curl, EZ Bar}] for overall size, and a machine curl ([{Bicep Curl, Leverage Machine}]).

**Legs**: First goes [{Leg Extension}], to pre-exhaust the quads. According to Yates, that way he can use less weight on [{Leg Press}] but fully exhaust the quads anyway, with less stress on the joints. The last quad exercise is [{Hack Squat}]. For hamstrings there's [{Lying Leg Curl}], [{Stiff Leg Deadlift}], and a one-leg curl done upright (Liftosaur doesn't have it by default, so in the code it's replaced with [{Seated Leg Curl}] as a second leg curl). And for calves - [{Standing Calf Raise, Leverage Machine}] and [{Seated Calf Raise, Leverage Machine}].

**Abs**: [{Crunch}] and [{Reverse Crunch}], 3 sets each, on days 1 and 4. These are the only exercises with more than one working set.

**Swaps**: Yates himself preferred machine variations where possible. But if the gym you go to has different equipment, you can replace Hammer Strength with any plate-loaded/selectorized version of the same exercise. And instead of the hack squat you could do [{Leg Press}] with a narrow stance, or [{Front Squat}] would work too.

### Set & Rep Scheme

Every exercise (except abs) follows the same pattern:

1. **0-3 warm-up sets** with increasing weight, not to failure. The first exercise for a muscle gets 1-3, later exercises for the same muscle get fewer, and a lot of them don't have any at all. The code uses the warm-ups Yates actually did - e.g. for [{Incline Bench Press}] he did 135 x 12, 220 x 10, 310 x 8, and then 425 x 8. If you convert those to % of the top set weight, you get 30%, 50% and 75%.
2. **A single max-effort set** to failure within the rep range.

Rep ranges from the book:

- **Majority of the upper body exercises, hack squats and leg curls**: 8-10 reps per set
- **Biceps, Hammer Strength chest press**: 6-8
- **Incline bench press, incline flyes, deadlift**: 8
- **Stiff-leg deadlift**: 10
- **Shrugs, back extensions, cable crossovers, leg extensions, leg press, calves**: 10-12
- **Abs**: 3 sets of 20-25 crunches, and 3 sets of 12-15 reverse crunches

**"Failure and beyond"**: Failure is pretty much where the set only starts to get serious. On the majority of main sets Yates did 2-3 forced reps with a workout partner, and sometimes added negatives / rest-pause reps on top of that. And the way he extended the set depended on the exercise:

- **Forced reps** (partner helps just enough so the bar keeps going) - presses, pulldowns, pushdowns, the majority of machine exercises
- **Rest-pause** (rack the weight for 10 sec, do 1 more rep, wait another 10 sec, do 1 more rep) - good if you lift alone
- **Negatives** (partner helps you lift it, then you lower it super slowly, the slower the better) - only on machine exercises, for safety
- **Partial reps** (3/4 reps, then half reps, then a quarter rep) - lateral raises and barbell rows, where forced reps don't really make sense
- **Drop sets** (grab lighter dumbbells and do 2-3 more reps) - dumbbell curls

Don't overdo it on small muscles. E.g. for biceps Yates only did 1-2 forced reps, and never pushed them as far past failure as back or legs.

**Tempo**: Lower the weight slowly and controlled, and lift it smoothly - don't bounce it or explode from the lowest point. For rows, squeeze for about a second in the top position, and also stop briefly in the lowest position on each rep.

### Progressive Overload

Double progression, one set at a time:

1. Choose the weight where you'd fail somewhere within the range of reps.
2. Each week, you go to failure with the same weight, and try to beat the reps from the previous week.
3. Once you hit the top of the range (e.g. 10 reps for the 8-10 exercise), increase the weight the following week: **5lb** for the majority of exercises, **10lb** for [{Deadlift}], [{Stiff Leg Deadlift}], [{Leg Press}], [{Hack Squat}] and [{Standing Calf Raise, Leverage Machine}].

The app does it for you - if you manage to do the top number of reps on the main set, it'll increase the weight for the exercise. If you do less reps than the bottom number, the weight stays the same, and you just repeat it the following week.

Only record the reps you did without any help. Forced reps, negatives and partials shouldn't count towards the top of the range, otherwise you'd bump the weight too early.

**When you plateau**: The program doesn't have deloads. Yates' approach to plateaus was just to recover better - get more hours in bed, eat even bigger, and if some muscle seems overtrained, skip it for a week. Sometimes Yates would drop the biceps workout completely, and not do any biceps for 2 weeks, since biceps get hit pretty hard on back day anyway. Also, according to Yates, weights are what you watch, not what you chase. The weight only matters as a way to track progress, and you never sacrifice form just to add 5lb.

### How Long to Run It / What Next

Yates was doing this routine for 3 years, and won 3 Olympias on it. There's not really a fixed end - you can keep running it while you still add reps or weight on the majority of exercises each couple weeks.

Signs it's time to change:

- Joints (elbows, shoulders, lower back) start to hurt, since you push each set all the way to the limit
- You can't add reps/weight on the majority of the exercises for 4+ weeks, even after taking a few more rest days
- You dread the workouts - you just can't push hard on the sets anymore

After it, you could switch to [Mike Mentzer's Heavy Duty](/programs/mike-mentzer-heavy-duty), or if you want to do even less sets - there's [Heavy Duty Consolidated Routine](/programs/mike-mentzer-consolidated). If you enjoy the intensity, but want to hit each muscle more often - check [DoggCrapp](/programs/doggcrapp). And if you also want higher frequency and more sets weekly - try [PHAT](/programs/phat) / [PHUL](/programs/phul).

### Equipment Needed

As written, the program is built for a fully equipped commercial gym:

- Regular bar, EZ bar, dumbbell set, flat/incline bench
- Smith machine (or you can just do [{Overhead Press, Dumbbell}] seated instead)
- Cable station (for unilateral laterals, pushdowns, crossovers, rows)
- Hammer Strength machines: pulldown, iso-row, chest press, rear delt (you can replace them with [{Pull Up}], [{Bent Over One Arm Row}], [{Bench Press, Dumbbell}], [{Reverse Fly}])
- Leg extension, leg press, hack squat, leg curl, standing/seated calf raise
- Hyperextension station

Home gym version (barbell, dumbbells, bench, pull-up bar):

- Cable laterals → another set of dumbbell [{Lateral Raise}], leaning to the side
- Pushdowns → [{Triceps Extension}] with a dumbbell, or [{Triceps Dip}]
- Pulldowns → [{Pull Up}] or [{Chin Up}]
- Machine and cable rows → [{Bent Over One Arm Row}] and [{Bent Over Row, Dumbbell}]
- Cable crossovers → [{Chest Fly}]
- Machine curl → [{Hammer Curl}]
- Leg extension + leg press + hack squat → [{Bulgarian Split Squat}], [{Front Squat}], [{Squat}]
- Leg curls → [{Nordic Curl}] and [{Romanian Deadlift}]
- Calf machines → single-leg [{Standing Calf Raise}] on a step, holding a dumbbell

### Rest Times

Yates didn't really specify the rest times for the routine. When talking about his early days in the gym, he said he'd only rest until he was ready to go again, and there wasn't any chatting while resting. And for his 6-week program for novices, Yates suggested 60-90 seconds. In the program we use:

- **120 seconds** after each warm-up and working set - long enough to give the next set 100%
- **60 seconds** between ab sets

Leg day is usually longer - after a really hard set of leg presses, you may need 3-4 minutes of rest until the following exercise. Biceps would be quicker.

Before every workout, Yates did 10 minutes on a stationary bike and then gentle stretching for the muscles he was about to train.

### How to Pick Starting Weights

The starting weights in the code are rough guesses for a lifter with 3+ years of training. You'll need to adjust them in the first 1-2 weeks:

1. For each exercise, choose the weight you think you'd fail with somewhere close to the lower end of the range, using strict form and doing slow reps.
2. If you can do way more reps and go well past the top of the range - note it, and add more weight next week. Don't wait until the progression slowly gets you there 5lb by 5lb.
3. The weights would be lower compared to usual for exercises that come later for the same muscle - e.g. leg press after leg extensions, deadlifts as the last exercise of back day, the third triceps exercise.

The warm-up sets are % of the working set weight, so they adjust automatically.

A common mistake is to just reuse the weights from other programs, where you stop 2-3 reps before failure. If you do slow reps and go to real failure, the weight would be lower than you expect, and that's okay. Yates himself switched from 6-8 to 8-10 reps in 1995, and also used less weight.

### Common Modifications

- **No training partner**: Instead of forced reps, you can do rest-pause - rack the weight at failure, wait 10 seconds, do 1 more rep, wait 10 seconds again, then 1 more rep. Or do drop sets on dumbbell and machine exercises.
- **Less experienced lifters**: Yates himself suggested to do 3 sets per exercise (first one is a warm-up), go to failure on the last 2, and skip forced reps and negatives. That's pretty close to the 2-set routine he did from 1986 to 1991.
- **Lower back fatigue**: Deadlifts to failure at the end of the back day are really hard to recover from. Some people replace them with [{Romanian Deadlift}], or just remove them and only keep back extensions.
- **More frequency**: If one time per week is not enough for you, you can do the 4 workouts over 5-6 days instead of 7.
- **Weak body parts**: Don't add more sets. Yates' answer was to execute them better and recover more, not more volume. If some body part is lagging, just do it first in the workout.

### What is Dorian Yates' Blood and Guts training?

This is the high-intensity approach Dorian Yates used to win six Mr. Olympia titles. You do max 3 warm-up sets (and skip them for the following exercises for the same muscle), then just a single all-out working set per exercise, to failure. Often you also go beyond that with forced reps, rest-pause or partials. The name comes from his 1993 book and 1996 workout video - both called "Blood & Guts".

### How many sets does Dorian Yates do per exercise?

It's just a single main set per exercise, after 0-3 warmups with less weight. Abs are the exception - those get 3. Prior to 1992 he did 2 main sets per exercise, and 3 back when he was a beginner.

### Is Blood and Guts good for beginners?

No, not really. Yates said it took him 9 years to start going 100% on a single set, and for novices he recommended 3 sets per exercise. He also didn't do forced reps before his second year of training. If you're a beginner, you'll get way better results if you focus on proper form with more sets and lower intensity.

### Can you do Blood and Guts without a training partner?

Yes, but you'd need to replace forced reps and negatives with something else. E.g. rest-pause sets (go to failure, wait 10 seconds, then squeeze another 1-2 reps), partial reps on raises and rows, and drop sets on dumbbell exercises. Yates specifically recommended rest-pause if you train alone.

### How often does Dorian Yates train each muscle?

Once a week. The program is 4 training days out of 7: delts/traps/triceps, back, chest/biceps, then legs, with rest days after the second, third and fourth workouts. Yates moved from training each body part every 5 days to every 7 days as he got stronger.

### Does the Dorian Yates workout work for natural lifters?

Yes, if you actually push the sets to real failure. Less sets and lots of rest between workouts is a good fit for lifters that recover slowly. But current research generally prefers hitting each muscle twice a week, and doing more sets in total. So some naturals would probably grow faster on a program that has more sets.

### When should I add weight on Blood and Guts?

If you hit the top of the range on the main set without any help (e.g. 10 on the 8-10 exercise) - bump the weight by 5lb, and by 10lb for big leg movements and deadlifts. Forced reps and partials don't count there.
